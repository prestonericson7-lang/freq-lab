freq-lab FPGA wiring

Your board has three designs ready to load. Each one turns the FPGA into a different lab instrument that you run from the PC over a 3-wire serial link. This sheet covers what each one does, what plugs into which pin, and what a working first run looks like.

The three designs

Lock-in amplifierStart here

A signal generator and a very sensitive meter in one.

Sends a sine wave into something, then measures how strongly and how late it answers at exactly that frequency, even when the answer is buried in noise. Sweep the frequency to draw a resonance curve, or let it lock onto a resonance and follow it.

First test
3 resistors and 1 capacitor
Later
coils, piezos, crystals, LC circuits, sensors
Photon counter

A two-channel pulse counter that spots coincidences.

Counts pulses from two light detectors, and counts how often both fire within a time window you set, in 20 ns steps. Subtracting the chance overlaps tells you whether both detectors are seeing the same light.

First test
any two 3.3 V pulse sources
Real use
two detector modules with 3.3 V pulse outputs
Two-channel correlator

Compares two analog signals in real time.

Samples two inputs about 195,000 times a second each and reports how alike they are (coherence, −1 to +1) and at what time offset. Built for two-station ELF antenna experiments; a GPS input lines up two boards.

First test
one signal into both inputs
Real use
two sensors with amplifiers, GPS optional

Do the lock-in loop-back first

It needs no sensors. Three resistors and a capacitor feed the board's own output back into its input, so one run proves the whole chain: loading the design, the serial link, the signal output, the ADC and the math. After that, anything that misbehaves is the sensor, not the setup. Then dry-run the photon counter with test pulses, then the correlator.

All three: build clean in Vivado 2026.1, timing met, simulation passes. None has run on the real board yet.

JM1 pin map

Lock-in uses JM1 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, plus GND.

  • Analog in (ADC)
  • Signal out
  • Digital in
  • Serial to PC
  • Fan
  • 3.3 V out
  • 5 V out
  • Ground
  • Free

Odd pins are one row and even pins the other; pin 2 sits right next to pin 1. Pin 1 is marked on the board (square pad, triangle or a "1" on the silkscreen). The small codes next to each pin (E17, H16…) are the FPGA ball names from Puzhi's pin sheet.

Every signal pin on JM1 is 3.3 V logic. Bank 35 can be switched to 1.8 or 2.5 V, but these designs assume the default 3.3 V. JM2 is not used.

Before you connect anything

  • 3.3 V maximum on any signal pin. JM1-1 is 5 V. Never let it touch a signal pin.
  • ADC pins read 0 to 1.0 V (JM1-6, 8, 9, 11). Above 1 V just reads full scale. Above 3.3 V can damage the chip.
  • Detector inputs take 3.3 V logic pulses only. Never wire a SiPM, APD or photomultiplier straight in. Their bias runs from tens of volts to over a thousand. A module with a built-in discriminator or comparator output is what goes here.
  • Serial is 3.3 V. Use the Teensy 4.1 bridge or a 3.3 V USB-serial adapter, never a 5 V one.
  • Check the fan's PWM wire first. Power the fan, leave its PWM wire unplugged, and measure it to ground. 3.3 V or less is fine. About 5 V needs a small transistor in between; don't connect it directly.
  • One shared ground. Teensy, sensors, detectors and GPS all connect to board GND (JM1-3, 4 or 33–36).
  • Rewire with the board unplugged.

Lock-in amplifier

Vivado: builds, timing met with 11.9 ns to spare, 3.6 % of the chip, 0.13 W · Simulation: amplitude within 0.1 %, phase within 0.4° · Hardware: not run yet

The FPGA generates a sine wave digitally and sends it out of JM1-10 as a very fast on/off stream. The 1 kΩ + 10 nF filter smooths that into a real sine, from micro-hertz steps up to about 100 kHz. Whatever comes back is sampled on JM1-6, 390,625 times a second.

The FPGA multiplies those samples by its own sine and cosine and averages them. Anything that isn't at the drive frequency, like noise and mains hum, averages toward zero. What's left is the amplitude and the phase lag of the response at exactly the drive frequency.

First test: loop-back

The board's output goes through the filter. The divider brings it under 1 V, and it comes straight back into the ADC. Add the PC link from the shared section below. Parts: 1 kΩ, 33 kΩ and 10 kΩ resistors, a 10 nF capacitor, a breadboard and jumpers.

Commands

From P:\Downloads\freq-lab, after pip install pyserial once. COM7 stands for your Teensy's port (Device Manager → Ports).

python tools/lockin_host.py COM7 id
python tools/lockin_host.py COM7 cal
python tools/lockin_host.py COM7 sweep 50 2000 40 --log --amp 0.2 --out loop.csv
python tools/lockin_host.py COM7 off

What working looks like

id prints the design's ID. cal measures the loop's delay and saves it. The sweep should then read almost the same amplitude at every frequency with a lag near 0°, because nothing in the loop resonates. If you get that, the whole chain works.

LEDs and sync

LED1 blinks about once a second while the design runs. LED2 toggles after every finished measurement. JM1-12 puts out a square wave at the drive frequency for a scope trigger.

Measuring something real

Drive the thing you're testing from the filter node, such as a coil, piezo or LC circuit. Bring its response back into JM1-6 through the analog front end in the correlator section. That holds the pin at 0.5 V and keeps the swing in range. Sweep across the range you care about, then track 440 --step 0.05 follows a resonance near 440 Hz as it drifts.

Photon coincidence counter

Vivado: builds, timing met with 11.6 ns to spare, 1.1 % of the chip, 0.11 W · Simulation: 12 of 12 checks · Hardware: not run yet
Fixed tonight: pulses that land in the same 20 ns tick now count as a coincidence (they used to be missed), and each pulse counts toward at most one pair.

Each detector sends one short 3.3 V pulse per photon. The FPGA checks both inputs every 20 ns and counts every pulse on each channel; these are the singles. When A and B fire within the window you set, in either order, it also counts a coincidence. The window is set in 20 ns ticks, so window 10 means within ±200 ns.

Two detectors watching unrelated light still line up by chance now and then. The accidental command works out how many of those to expect from the singles. The coincidences left over are photons hitting both detectors together.

Power the detector modules from their own supply and share ground with the board. For a dry run without detectors, any two 3.3 V pulse sources work: a function generator, or a spare Teensy toggling two pins. The gate input has an internal pull-up, so leaving it unconnected means counting is on.

Commands

This one is interactive: start it with just the port, then type at the photon> prompt.

python tools/photon_host.py COM7
photon> window 10      # within ±200 ns
photon> run 10         # count 10 s, print results
photon> accidental     # chance overlaps vs real
photon> csv run1.csv 60 5
photon> quit

What working looks like

It prints the design's ID as soon as it connects. run 10 prints singles A, singles B, coincidences and elapsed time. In a dry run, pulses sent to both inputs at the same moment give coincidences equal to the singles, while two unrelated pulse trains give about the accidental number.

LED1 blinks about once a second. LED2 is on while counting.

Two-channel correlator

Vivado: builds, timing met with 8.1 ns to spare, 2.4 % of the chip, 0.12 W · Simulation: 7 of 7 · Hardware: not run yet
Fixed tonight: the ADC now samples your two inputs (it was set to read the chip's own supply voltage), channel B moved to JM1-9/11 (the old pins weren't ADC pins), and the ADC's input wiring inside the chip was corrected.

The on-chip ADC samples channel A and channel B in turn, about 195,000 times a second each. Over each window it adds up A², B², A×B and the averages, and the PC turns that into a coherence between −1 and +1.

Two sensors picking up the same distant source read high, for example two ELF antennas hearing the Earth–ionosphere (Schumann) resonances. Noise that's local to each sensor doesn't line up and reads near 0. A GPS module's once-a-second pulse lets two boards at different places line up their sample counts.

Channel B is on the odd row: JM1-9 and JM1-11. Older notes said 10 and 12, but those aren't ADC pins. With nothing on JM1-18 it reads low, so the GPS input is safe to leave empty.

Analog front end (one per ADC input)

56 kΩ to 3.3 V and 10 kΩ to ground hold the ADC pin at 0.5 V, the middle of its 0 to 1 V range. Capacitor C passes the sensor's signal and blocks its DC, and it sets the lowest frequency that gets through. 100 nF passes above about 190 Hz, which is fine for the lock-in. For ELF signals use 220 µF non-polarized, which passes down to about 0.1 Hz; give it about 10 s after power-up to settle. For channel B, use JM1-9 (+) and JM1-11 (−).

Commands

Interactive, like the photon counter: start with the port, then type at corr>.

python tools/correlator_host.py COM7
corr> adc             # both ≈ 2048 on the bias
corr> window 100000   # ≈ 0.5 s per window
corr> stream 5        # coherence of 5 windows
corr> lagplot 16      # offsets ±16 × 5.12 µs
corr> quit

What working looks like

adc shows both channels near 2048 (0.5 V) with just the front ends connected. With the same signal into both channels, stream 5 prints coherence close to +1.000. Two unrelated signals give close to 0.

For ELF work use long windows, because the first Schumann mode is 7.8 Hz: window 1000000 is about 5 s. stream waits at most 10 s per window, so stay under about 1,900,000.

LED1 blinks about once a second. LED2 is on while a window is being captured.

Known limit: lagplot only uses the last 256 samples and wraps around at the ends, so values away from lag 0 are a little off. Coherence at lag 0 is exact.

PC link, fan and loading a design

Serial link to the PC

Load teensy/fpga_uart_bridge onto the Teensy and the PC sees the FPGA as the Teensy's COM port (115200 baud). The wires cross: FPGA TX goes to Teensy RX, and Teensy TX goes to FPGA RX. A 3.3 V USB-serial adapter works the same way: its RX to JM1-14, its TX to JM1-16, and GND to GND.

Fan

All three designs run the fan the same way: 25 kHz PWM on JM1-5, with the tach read on JM1-7 (the FPGA has a pull-up on it). Hold KEY1 on the board for full speed. A 12 V fan needs its own 12 V supply with its ground tied to the board's. Measure the PWM wire first (see the safety list).

Loading a design onto the board

  1. Wire everything with the board unplugged, then power it.
  2. Set the boot-mode jumpers to JTAG.
  3. Plug the board's JTAG USB-C into the PC. It's the port wired to the onboard FT232H and shows up as an FTDI device. The other USB-C is a CH340 serial port to the ARM side, which these designs don't use.
  4. Open Vivado 2026.1 Tcl Shell from the Start menu and type the two lines below, using the folder of the design you want.
  5. LED1 starts blinking about once a second, so it's running. A design loaded over JTAG lasts until power-off, so load it again after a power cycle.
cd P:/Downloads/freq-lab/fpga/lockin      # or fpga/photon_counter, fpga/correlator
source program.tcl

To rebuild after changing any HDL, double-click build_all_vivado.bat in P:\Downloads\freq-lab\fpga. It rebuilds all three, about 8 minutes.
